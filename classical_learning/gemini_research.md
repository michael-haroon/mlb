When a batter has limited plate appearances (e.g., a rookie with 15 at-bats), their raw batting average is highly volatile. Rather than using this raw average or a flat league average, Empirical Bayes pulls the estimate toward a data-driven prior, with the level of shrinkage dictated by the player's sample size.For binomial rate statistics—such as a hitter's walk rate ($BB/PA$), strikeout rate ($K/PA$), or batting average ($H/AB$)—the true latent rate $\theta_j$ for player $j$ is assumed to be drawn from a global Beta distribution prior:$$\theta_j \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$$[cite: 16, 18]The parameters $\alpha_0$ and $\beta_0$ represent the prior pseudo-counts of successes and failures. To estimate these hyperparameters without lookahead bias, one performs maximum likelihood estimation (via a beta-binomial regression) strictly on the historical cohort of players from previous seasons. Once the prior distribution $\text{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$ is established, the player's posterior estimate is updated using their specific historical data:$$\theta_j \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$$[cite: 16]This formulation balances a player's raw performance with their sample size: as the player's at-bats ($AB_j$) increase, the weight of the global prior diminishes, allowing the posterior estimate to converge to their true talent level.For continuous, heteroskedastic pitching metrics such as FIP or WHIP, a normal-normal conjugate framework is applied. The Empirical Bayes estimator shrinks the observed mean $\bar{y}_j$ toward the grand mean $\hat{\mu}$ using a sample-size-dependent shrinkage factor $B_j$:$$\hat{\theta}_j^{\text{EB}} = (1 - B_j)\bar{y}_j + B_j\hat{\mu}$$[cite: 13]The shrinkage factor $B_j$ is defined as:$$\hat{\theta}_j^{\text{EB}} = (1 - B_j)\bar{y}_j + B_j\hat{\mu}$$[cite: 13]where $\sigma_j^2 = s_j^2 / n_j$ represents the sampling variance of the player's observations (which scales inversely with their sample size $n_j$), and $\hat{\tau}^2$ is the estimated between-group variance denoting the true underlying dispersion of talent across the league.If a rookie pitcher has very few innings pitched, $\sigma_j^2$ is large relative to $\hat{\tau}^2$, driving $B_j$ close to $1.0$ and collapsing their estimate toward the grand mean. For an established starter, $\sigma_j^2$ is small, leaving their raw average virtually unshrunk.  ======================================================================================

Kalman Filtering for Latent Performance TrackingWhile Empirical Bayes shrinkage provides a robust static prior, a player's underlying talent level is dynamic and shifts due to physical aging, mechanical changes, or undisclosed injuries. To separate transient observational noise from a player's underlying talent trajectory over time, the pipeline can implement a state-space Kalman Filter. Under this formulation, a pitcher's true latent pitching quality ($x_t$, represented as a standardized ERA or WHIP) is modeled as a hidden state, while their observed performance ($y_t$) is a noisy realization:$$\text{State Equation: } x_{t+1} = x_t + w_t, \quad w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{process}})$$[cite: 14, 15]$$\text{Observation Equation: } y_t = x_t + v_t, \quad v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{obs}})$$[cite: 14, 15]The term $w_t$ represents the process noise (the gradual drift in a pitcher's actual talent level), and $v_t$ represents the observation noise (the game-to-game variance caused by defense, sequencing luck, and umpire decisions).The Kalman Filter updates the player's state mean and covariance recursively using only data available prior to game $t$. During forecasting, when future observations are unavailable, predictions are generated by propagating the state forward using the process model:$$\hat{x}_{t+1|t} = \hat{x}_{t|t}$$[cite: 14, 15]This formulation allows the system to differentiate a sustained drop in true pitching quality (e.g., a starter whose velocity has steadily declined) from game-to-game statistical noise.Advanced Pregame Feature Engineering FrameworkTo overcome the low-predictivity bounds of the pregame model suite, feature engineering must transcend basic historical averages and incorporate the physical, situational, and temporal realities of Major League Baseball.Dynamic Pitcher fatigue and Workload IndicatorsThe conventional approach of calculating rolling bullpen ERAs ignores the non-linear relationship between reliever workload, high-stress sequences, and performance degradation. This metric quantifies bullpen fatigue, providing linear models with a direct signal for high-variance bullpen matchups.Times Through the Order (TTO) Risk MetricA starting pitcher's effectiveness degrades systematically due to muscle fatigue and the batter's visual adaptation during a game. Derivation of Game Winner, Spreads, and Run LinesThe run differential $D = Y_{\text{home}} - Y_{\text{away}}$ is modeled as the difference of two correlated negative binomial variables. Under this joint formulation, the probability of a Home Win is calculated by summing the joint probabilities over all positive differentials:$$P(\text{Home Win}) = \sum_{y_1=1}^{\infty} \sum_{y_2=0}^{y_1-1} P(Y_{\text{home}} = y_1, Y_{\text{away}} = y_2)$$[cite: 7]Similarly, the probability that the home team covers a spread $S$ (e.g., $-1.5$ runs) is derived via:$$P(\text{Home Win}) = \sum_{y_1=1}^{\infty} \sum_{y_2=0}^{y_1-1} P(Y_{\text{home}} = y_1, Y_{\text{away}} = y_2)$$[cite: 7, 30]This approach is mathematically superior to directly fitting classification models on binary win/loss targets. Hierarchical Attention Networks (HAN)A baseball game is not a flat sequence of independent pitches. It is a strict fractal hierarchy: pitches compose plate appearances (PA), PAs compose half-innings, and half-innings compose games. A HAN explicitly enforces this structure, computing representations at each discrete level of the sport's hierarchy:$$\mathbf{v}_{pa} = \text{Attention}(\text{Encoder}_{pitch}(\mathbf{X}_{pitches}))$$[cite: 24]The input tensor is reshaped from a flat sequence format into a structurally padded 5D tensor:$$\mathbf{v}_{pa} = \text{Attention}(\text{Encoder}_{pitch}(\mathbf{X}_{pitches}))$$[cite: 24]where $B$ is the batch size, $I$ is the number of innings, $A$ is the max at-bats per inning, $P$ is the max pitches per at-bat, and $F$ is the feature dimension. This model is highly effective for inning-level targets like yrfi and first five innings winner, as the intermediate representations can be directly extracted to predict outcomes at specific stages of the game.2. Selective State Space Models (Mamba)While Transformers exhibit quadratic complexity $\mathcal{O}(S^2)$ over long sequences (which dilutes attention weights over pitch sequences containing hundreds of entries), Mamba models use Selective State Spaces to achieve linear scaling $\mathcal{O}(S)$. The continuous-time state space model is formulated as:$$h'(t) = \mathbf{A}h(t) + \mathbf{B}x(t)$$[cite: 24]$$y(t) = \mathbf{C}h(t)$$[cite: 24]Mamba makes the matrices $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{C}$ data-dependent (selective). This allows the model to "forget" irrelevant pitches (e.g., a foul ball with no baserunners) and "remember" critical state shifts (e.g., a starting pitcher's velocity dropping, signaling fatigue). The model operates directly on flat $(B, S, F)$ sequence tensors, serving as a high-speed replacement for LSTM and Transformer sequence encoders.3. Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs)Baseball sequences are asynchronous. The real-world time elapsed between pitches varies from 15 seconds to several minutes during pitching changes or delays. Neural ODEs treat time as a continuous variable by parameterizing the derivative of the hidden state:$$\mathbf{h}_{t_i} = \mathbf{h}_{t_{i-1}} + \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i} f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}(t), t) dt$$[cite: 24]This formulation allows the model's hidden states to decay or shift naturally over time, capturing continuous physical realities like a pitcher's arm cooling down during a long half-inning or rain delay.4.     def __init__(self, game_pk: str, api_base_url: str = "https://statsapi.mlb.com"):
        self.game_pk = game_pk
        self.api_base_url = api_base_url
        self.state = LiveGameState(game_pk=game_pk)
        self.http_session: Optional[aiohttp.ClientSession] = None
        self.running = False

    async def initialize(self):
        """Initializes the HTTP session and retrieves the complete starting GUMBO payload."""
        self.http_session = aiohttp.ClientSession()
        logger.info("Fetching baseline GUMBO state for game %s", self.game_pk)
        url = f"{self.api_base_url}/api/v1.1/game/{self.game_pk}/feed/live"
        async with self.http_session.get(url) as response:
            if response.status != 200:
                raise RuntimeError(f"Failed to fetch baseline GUMBO: HTTP {response.status}")
            data = await response.json()
            async with self.state.lock:
                self.state.gumbo_data = data
                self.state.last_update_id = data.get("metaData", {}).get("timestamp")
                logger.info("Baseline state established at timestamp: %s", self.state.last_update_id)

    async def apply_diff_patch(self, patch_payload: Dict[str, Any]) -> bool:
        """Applies an incoming JSON Patch payload to the local GUMBO cache."""
        async with self.state.lock:
            try:
                patch = jsonpatch.JsonPatch(patch_payload.get("diff", []))
                patched_gumbo = patch.apply(self.state.gumbo_data)
                
                new_timestamp = patch_payload.get("metaData", {}).get("timestamp")
                self.state.gumbo_data = patched_gumbo
                self.state.last_update_id = new_timestamp
                logger.debug("Successfully applied JSON patch. Timestamp updated to %s", new_timestamp)
                return True
            except Exception as patch_error:
                logger.error("Failed to apply JSON patch to game %s: %s", self.game_pk, str(patch_error))
                return False

    async def fetch_full_fallback(self):
        """Fallback to pull the complete GUMBO model on patch sync failure."""
        logger.info("Executing full GUMBO fallback fetch for game %s", self.game_pk)
        url = f"{self.api_base_url}/api/v1.1/game/{self.game_pk}/feed/live"
        try:
            async with self.http_session.get(url) as response:
                if response.status == 200:
                    data = await response.json()
                    async with self.state.lock:
                        self.state.gumbo_data = data
                        self.state.last_update_id = data.get("metaData", {}).get("timestamp")
                        logger.info("Successfully resynchronized full GUMBO at timestamp: %s", self.state.last_update_id)
                else:
                    logger.error("Fallback pull failed with status: %d", response.status)
        except Exception as network_error:
            logger.critical("Network failure during full resynchronization fallback: %s", str(network_error))

    async def extract_pitch_features(self) -> Dict[str, Any]:

                        break

            coordinates = pitch_data.get("coordinates", {})

                "balls": linescore.get("balls", 0),
                "strikes": linescore.get("strikes", 0),
                "outs": linescore.get("outs", 0),
                "inning": linescore.get("currentInning", 1),

                "runs_away": linescore.get("teams", {}).get("away", {}).get("runs", 0),
                "runner_on_first": 1 if linescore.get("offense", {}).get("first") else 0,
                "runner_on_second": 1 if linescore.get("offense", {}).get("second") else 0,
                "runner_on_third": 1 if linescore.get("offense", {}).get("third") else 0,
                "pitch_velocity": pitch_data.get("startSpeed", 0.0),
                "pitch_spin_rate": pitch_data.get("spinRate", 0.0),
                "plate_x": coordinates.get("pX", 0.0),
                "plate_z": coordinates.get("pZ", 0.0),
            }
            return features

        self.running = True

            "topic": f"game-{self.game_pk}"
        }

            try:
                async with websockets.connect(ws_uri) as ws:

Quantifying Latent Game States: An Architectural Framework for High-Frequency MLB Prediction Market TradingPredictive accuracy in high-frequency sports prediction markets like Kalshi depends on resolving discrepancies between static pregame expectations and dynamic, live-game event updates. Traditional sportbooks build a structural margin into their pricing, but binary contract markets converge strictly to $1.00 or $0.00 at settlement, turning probability calibration into the primary driver of long-term capital allocation.To secure a mathematical edge, a trading pipeline must integrate a highly structured pregame prior engine with a sub-second, low-latency live-game execution framework.This report provides a comprehensive blueprint for modeling MLB game outcomes. It details the mathematical optimization of pregame target structures, outlines the engineering of a live-game data ingestion system utilizing Major League Baseball's Grand Unified Master Baseball Object (GUMBO) API, and proposes a double-calibrated deep learning stacking architecture designed to operate at scale.Pregame Target Optimization and Hierarchical Joint ModelingThe pregame module historically experienced a significant performance degradation when modeling away_runs ($A$) and home_runs ($H$) as independent regression targets. These direct targets performed approximately five times worse than derived targets such as total_runs ($T = H + A$) and runs_diff ($D = H - A$). This degradation stems from treating dependent, over-dispersed count variables as symmetric, continuous, and statistically isolated outcomes.In an actual baseball game, team scoring processes are coupled by shared physical parameters, including local weather, park factors, and game-state dynamics. Direct regression models minimize univariate loss functions like Mean Absolute Error (MAE) but discard the cross-sectional correlation and tail-dependence of the scores.To resolve this structural deficiency, the target architecture must be reframed. Instead of estimating $H$ and $A$ via isolated regression models, the pipeline must estimate the joint probability mass function $P(H = h, A = a)$. This joint distribution preserves the correlation structure and allows the system to derive exact probability distributions for total runs and run differentials. These derived metrics map directly to the strike thresholds of active prediction market contracts.Joint Probability Estimation via Discrete Copula FrameworksTo capture the interdependence of team scoring without assuming a restrictive joint distribution, the pipeline implements a bivariate copula framework with discrete marginal distributions. Baseball runs are count data characterized by over-dispersion, where the variance of scoring is significantly larger than the mean. Consequently, the marginal distributions for home runs ($F_H$) and away runs ($F_A$) are modeled using Negative Binomial distributions rather than Poisson distributions.Let $H \sim \text{NegBin}(\mu_H, \alpha_H)$ and $A \sim \text{NegBin}(\mu_A, \alpha_A)$, where $\mu$ represents the mean scoring rate and $\alpha$ is the dispersion parameter. According to Sklar’s theorem, any bivariate cumulative distribution function $F(h, a)$ can be expressed in terms of its marginals and a copula $C$:$$F(h, a) = C(F_H(h), F_A(a); \theta)$$where $\theta$ is the copula parameter capturing the dependency structure.Because count data are discrete, the copula is not uniquely defined on the entire unit square $[0, 1]^2$, but it is uniquely determined on the range of the marginal cumulative distribution functions. To evaluate the joint probability mass function (PMF) from a continuous copula, the system applies finite forward differences:$$P(H = h, A = a) = F(h, a) - F(h - 1, a) - F(h, a - 1) + F(h - 1, a - 1)$$For modeling baseball scores, the Frank copula and the Gaussian copula are selected as the primary dependency structures. The Frank copula is particularly suitable because it is symmetric and permits both positive and negative dependence, which is essential for capturing matchups that range from highly defensive pitch-offs to asymmetric blowouts.The Frank copula is defined as:$$C_{\theta}^{F}(u, v) = -\frac{1}{\theta} \ln\left( 1 + \frac{(e^{-\theta u} - 1)(e^{-\theta v} - 1)}{e^{-\theta} - 1} \right)$$where $\theta \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$.An alternative formulation is the Skellam distribution, which models the difference between two independent Poisson-distributed variables. If $H \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_H)$ and $A \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_A)$, the run differential $D = H - A$ follows a Skellam distribution with PMF:$$P(D = d) = e^{-(\lambda_H + \lambda_A)} \left( \frac{\lambda_H}{\lambda_A} \right)^{d/2} I_{|d|}\left(2\sqrt{\lambda_H \lambda_A}\right)$$where $I_{|d|}$ is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.However, the standard Skellam distribution assumes complete statistical independence between the scoring processes. In MLB games, this independence assumption is violated by shared exogenous factors like cold weather or a high-altitude venue like Coors Field.To estimate the parameters of the copula model under discrete marginals, the pipeline employs a two-stage inference approach.
